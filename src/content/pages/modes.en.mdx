The three **Build a Hooper modes** use the same thirteen-attribute draft and the same 82-game career test, but they change the information available before every decision. Classic shows the position and player ratings. Blind reveals the position but hides ratings. Chaos hides both the position and ratings until the final reveal. Those differences completely change how safely you can specialize, when you should spend rerolls, and which [Build a Hooper attributes](/attributes) are worth locking early.

This Build a Hooper modes guide explains the rules, difficulty, ideal player type, draft phases, common mistakes, and progression path for Classic, Blind, and Chaos. New players should begin with the [complete how-to-play guide](/how-to-play), then use this page to choose the right level of uncertainty.

## Build a Hooper modes quick comparison

| Mode | Position information | Ratings during draft | Team rerolls | Best use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Classic** | You choose PG, SG, SF, PF, or C | Visible | 3 | Learn the draft, test position builds, and repair weaknesses |
| **Blind** | Position is revealed before drafting | Hidden until final reveal | 1 | Practice role-based decisions without grade shopping |
| **Chaos** | Position is hidden until the end | Hidden until final reveal | 1 | Build adaptable players and chase the hardest uncertainty-based runs |

All Build a Hooper modes follow the same core loop:

1. Choose a mode.
2. Set or reveal the position according to the mode.
3. Spin a team-season.
4. Choose one player and lock one unused attribute.
5. Repeat until all thirteen attributes are filled.
6. Reveal the complete player and overall rating.
7. Join a career team, simulate 82 games, and enter the playoffs.

The mode changes your information, not the number of attributes or the basic season structure.

## Classic mode: complete draft information

Classic is the best starting point among the Build a Hooper modes because it lets you understand cause and effect. You choose a position, see every player’s ratings, and receive three rerolls. That makes Classic the clearest environment for learning which grades support a role and which attractive grades create an unbalanced card.

### What Classic mode shows

- Your chosen position from the beginning.
- Visible grades for every available player.
- Open and completed attribute slots.
- Three team rerolls for boards that do not fit the plan.

### Best Classic strategy

Classic rewards a position-first plan. Before the first spin, decide whether you are building a floor general, shot creator, 3-and-D wing, stretch four, paint anchor, or another clear role. Use visible ratings to establish the primary cluster during rounds 1–5, then repair the largest weakness before the final rounds.

For example, a point guard should normally secure HAN and PAS before chasing luxury ratings. A center should establish REB and IDEF before attempting a specialty 3PT or PAS route. The [best builds by position](/best-builds) page provides complete priorities for all five positions.

### How to use Classic rerolls

Classic gives three rerolls, but that does not mean you should use them immediately. Save at least one for the second half of the draft. Good reasons to reroll include:

- The roster has no useful rating in your primary cluster.
- Every remaining option duplicates a completed attribute.
- The board forces a pick that would leave a position-critical weakness.
- You are building a specialist and the spin offers no player compatible with that role.

Do not reroll simply because the best available rating is a B+ rather than an A. Across an 82-game season, a coherent B+/A- player can outperform a collection of unrelated A+ grades.

### Common Classic mistake: visible-grade greed

Classic looks easy because every letter is visible, but visibility creates a trap. Players start taking the largest grade on every spin instead of finishing a basketball role. A shooting guard collects DNK, REB, and BLK because they are A+, then reaches round ten without a real shooting skill. A center chases 3PT and PAS while leaving REB weak. Classic gives enough information to avoid those mistakes, but it cannot stop you from making them.

## Blind mode: position known, ratings hidden

Blind is the middle step in the Build a Hooper modes progression. You know whether the player is a PG, SG, SF, PF, or C, but every rating stays hidden until the final reveal. You also receive only one reroll. Blind removes grade shopping while preserving the most important piece of context: the position’s job.

### What Blind mode tests

Blind tests whether you understand player roles and positional priorities without relying on visible letters. When a roster appears, you must choose a player based on the type of basketball skills that player is likely to contribute, the attributes still open, and the identity you are trying to build.

A known lead guard is a safer HAN or PAS donor than a pure interior big. A recognized 3-and-D wing is a reasonable source for 3PT or PDEF. A glass-first big is a safer REB or IDEF choice. The ratings may surprise you, but the role gives you a rational decision process.

### Best Blind strategy

Start with the position’s non-negotiable responsibilities:

- **PG:** HAN and PAS, then one scoring axis.
- **SG:** 3PT or MID, FIN, then enough HAN.
- **SF:** 3PT, PDEF, FIN or ATH.
- **PF:** REB, IDEF, then FIN, STR, or 3PT depending on route.
- **C:** REB and IDEF, then BLK, FIN, or STR.

Because grades are hidden, do not build a plan that requires thirteen perfect outcomes. Blind builds should have a primary role and a backup route. A PG draft that misses passing can pivot toward a scoring lead guard. A stretch-four plan that misses 3PT can become an interior enforcer if REB, IDEF, FIN, and STR develop naturally.

### How to use the single Blind reroll

The Blind reroll should fix a structurally bad board, not a disappointing name. Spend it when every roster option is incompatible with an essential open attribute. If you have nine rounds remaining, patience is usually better than panic. If you have three rounds remaining and the position still lacks its central job, the reroll becomes much more valuable.

### Common Blind mistake: pretending hidden grades are visible

Blind players often select a famous scorer and assume every scoring attribute will be elite. That defeats the point of the mode. Treat the player’s known role as evidence, not certainty. Build several connected skills instead of betting the entire run on one assumed rating.

## Chaos mode: position and ratings hidden

Chaos is the hardest of the Build a Hooper modes because it removes both forms of security. You do not know the position, ratings remain hidden, and you receive one reroll. The final position can transform a seemingly strong specialist into a badly mismatched player. Chaos therefore rewards portability more than precision.

### What Chaos mode tests

Chaos tests whether the player you assemble can survive several possible position reveals. A pure movement shooter may look logical for SG but become fragile if the reveal is C. A paint-only REB/IDEF/BLK build may dominate at center but feel incomplete at PG. The safest Chaos strategy creates a versatile base before adding specialty ratings.

### Transferable Chaos attributes

The following combinations travel well across positions:

- **FIN + ATH:** useful for guards, wings, and bigs.
- **HAN + PDEF:** supports guard or wing outcomes.
- **REB + IDEF:** strong PF/C insurance and still useful for a physical wing.
- **3PT + FIN:** provides two scoring levels for several positions.
- **PAS + HAN:** useful for guards and point-forward or hub-big outcomes.

No combination is completely position-proof. The goal is not to eliminate risk; it is to avoid a build that works at only one position.

### Best Chaos strategy

During rounds 1–4, take broad skills rather than a narrow identity. By rounds 5–8, look at the clusters you have already formed and choose the most plausible range of positions. If REB and IDEF are developing, lean toward a big-capable build. If HAN, 3PT, and PDEF are developing, lean toward a guard/wing profile. During rounds 9–13, fill the most dangerous universal holes: no finishing, no creation, no defense, or no rebounding at all.

### Common Chaos mistake: copying a Classic build

A Classic strategy assumes the position is known and ratings can be targeted. Copying that exact plan into Chaos ignores the core rule of the mode. Chaos does not reward perfect adherence to a point guard or center checklist; it rewards a player who still makes sense when the reveal changes the job.

## Build a Hooper modes by draft phase

### Rounds 1–4: establish direction

- **Classic:** lock two or three position-defining ratings.
- **Blind:** choose donors whose roles match the known position.
- **Chaos:** favor transferable attributes and avoid extreme specialization.

The early phase should create direction, not complete the entire player. Do not waste rerolls trying to force a perfect opening.

### Rounds 5–9: complete the identity

- **Classic:** finish the primary cluster and choose a secondary strength.
- **Blind:** confirm whether the initial role remains realistic; pivot if the draft has consistently offered another path.
- **Chaos:** identify the likely position range and protect against the worst reveal.

This is usually the most important phase in all Build a Hooper modes. By round nine, you should be able to describe what the player does, even if Blind or Chaos ratings are still hidden.

### Rounds 10–13: repair and close

The final rounds should fill holes rather than create a new identity. CLU, ATH, STR, secondary defense, or a missing scoring level can complete a build. Do not spend the last locks doubling down on a strength while a position-critical category remains empty.

## Which Build a Hooper mode should you play first?

Follow this progression:

1. **Classic until you can build a clear role without constantly checking a guide.**
2. **Blind until you can use player roles and position priorities instead of visible grades.**
3. **Chaos when you are comfortable adapting to incomplete information and accepting variance.**

Classic is not merely an easy mode. It is the best laboratory for testing [point guard](/best-builds/point-guard), [shooting guard](/best-builds/shooting-guard), [small forward](/best-builds/small-forward), [power forward](/best-builds/power-forward), and [center](/best-builds/center) build plans. Blind teaches disciplined role recognition. Chaos teaches adaptability.

## Build a Hooper modes FAQ

### Do all modes use thirteen attributes?

Yes. Classic, Blind, and Chaos all use the same thirteen Build a Hooper attributes. Only position information, rating visibility, and reroll count change.

### Which mode is easiest?

Classic is easiest to understand because the position and ratings are visible and three rerolls are available. It is still possible to build a weak player by chasing unrelated grades.

### Which mode is hardest?

Chaos is generally hardest because both the position and ratings are hidden until the end. A strong Chaos draft needs adaptable attribute combinations.

### Is Blind just Chaos with a position?

The known position is a major difference. Blind lets you follow a position-specific responsibility list. Chaos requires protection against multiple possible positions.

### When should I use a reroll?

Use a reroll when the entire roster cannot support an essential open attribute or when the remaining rounds are too few to recover from the board. Do not reroll only because one preferred player is missing.

### Can I use the same best build in every mode?

The role can stay similar, but the execution should change. Classic permits precise specialization, Blind requires role-based donor choices, and Chaos needs more portable ratings.
